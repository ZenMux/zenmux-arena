\begin{abstract}
\noindent
When asked point-blank ``who are you?'', will a large language model truthfully name
the vendor that trained it, or will it claim to be another company's product? We answer
this with a large-scale, reproducible experiment: on the ZenMux platform we select
\statModels{} \emph{first-party}, frontier models from \statVendors{} vendors, ask each
of them repeatedly in \statLangs{} languages under three ``identity-probing'' variants,
collect \textbf{\statTotal{} answers} in total, and then use an independent extractor
model (\name{GPT-5.5}) to label each answer with the vendor it \emph{claims} to belong
to. Overall, models identify themselves correctly in \pc{\statSelf} of answers,
exhibit \emph{cross-vendor confusion} (claiming to be a different real vendor;
\statTotalCross{} answers) in \pc{\statCross}, refuse in \pc{\statRefused}, and give no
identity in \pc{\statUnknown}. The confusion is far from uniform: it concentrates in a
handful of vendors and is amplified along two clear axes --- \textbf{prompt intensity}
and \textbf{language}. A ``de-branding'' jailbreak-style prompt raises the cross-vendor
rate from \pc{\statBareCross} under the bare question to \pc{\statDebrandCross}
(roughly \emph{3.6$\times$}); French, Japanese, and Spanish show markedly more confusion
than Simplified Chinese. The targets of the confusion are not random either: it is
strongly \emph{directional}. Netting each vendor's ``imitated'' count against its
``imitates-others'' count, \name{Anthropic} (net \statAnthroNet{}),
\name{OpenAI} (\statOpenaiNet{}), and \name{Google} (\statGoogleNet{}) are net-inflow
``identity gravity centers'' that jointly absorb \pc{\statAnchorsShare} of all
cross-vendor claims, while \name{Tencent} (net \statTencentNet{}),
\name{Doubao} (\statDoubaoNet{}), and others are net-outflow ``imitators''. We analyze
the data at both the vendor and the individual-model level, and discuss what these
results mean for identity distillation, system-prompt overrides, and model-trust
evaluation.
\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} large language models; identity; self-identification;
cross-vendor confusion; jailbreak prompting; multilingual evaluation; data distillation
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

``Who are you?'' --- one of the most basic things you can ask a conversational system ---
unexpectedly reveals deep traces of a large language model's (LLM's) training history.
Ideally, a model trained by company A and served as company A's product should stably
identify itself as A's model. But frontier-model training increasingly relies on other
models' outputs: synthetic data, instruction distillation, and the ubiquitous
model-generated text in web corpora all pour \emph{identity statements} like ``I am
ChatGPT'' or ``I am Claude'' into a new model's weights. When those traces are strong
enough --- and the vendor has not fully overridden them via the system prompt or the
alignment stage --- the model ``lets it slip'' when asked about its identity, claiming
to be another company's product. We call this phenomenon \textbf{cross-vendor identity
confusion}.

This is more than a curiosity. It is research-relevant on at least three levels:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A forensic signal about data provenance.} Which vendor a model claims to
  be is a weak but real fingerprint of its training-data composition. A systematic
  pattern of ``vendor A's model claims to be vendor B'' is circumstantial evidence of a
  distillation relationship.
  \item \textbf{Reliability and trustworthiness of identity.} If a user-facing product
  misreports its own identity \pc{50} of the time, any downstream assumption that ``the
  model truthfully describes its own capabilities and provenance'' needs rethinking.
  \item \textbf{The fragility of system-prompt overrides.} Vendors routinely use the
  system prompt to \emph{stipulate} a model's external identity. A prompt that bypasses
  that layer and elicits the ``underlying self-report'' measures exactly how thin that
  override is.
\end{itemize}

Because LLMs are \emph{probabilistic} generators, a single answer to a single prompt is
not enough --- one ``slip of the tongue'' might just be sampling noise. Our design
therefore puts \textbf{repetition} at the center: each model is asked independently
30--40 times per language per variant, so that each model is queried exactly 1{,}100
times in total. At this sample size, a model's self-identification rate is no longer an
anecdote but a stable frequency estimate of its ``identity cognition.''

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) We build and release a large-scale identity self-report dataset spanning
\statVendors{} vendors, \statModels{} first-party frontier models, \statLangs{}
languages, three prompt variants, and \statTotal{} answers in total;
(2) we propose and quantify two \emph{amplification axes} of identity confusion ---
prompt intensity (\S\ref{sec:variant}) and language (\S\ref{sec:language}) --- and give
a per-model ``language fragility'' metric (the cross-language span of a model's
self-rate);
(3) we characterize the \emph{directionality} of confusion at the vendor level via an
\emph{identity net} (imitated minus imitates-others), showing it is highly asymmetric
and flows almost one-way toward a few ``gravity center'' vendors
(\S\ref{sec:vendor-overall}), and drill down to the model level (\S\ref{sec:per-vendor});
(4) we document several striking cases --- including one vendor that refuses identity
questions nearly \pc{100} of the time, and ``code-specialized'' sub-models that
systematically expose external identities more than their general-purpose siblings.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overall design}\label{sec:design}
The experiment is a three-way full-factorial design:
$\text{model} \times \text{language} \times \text{prompt variant}$, with repeated
sampling per cell. Its nominal size is
\[
\underbrace{3}_{\text{variants}} \times \underbrace{40}_{\text{repeats}} \times
\underbrace{10}_{\text{languages}} \times \underbrace{27}_{\text{models}} = 32{,}400
\ \text{answers},
\]
i.e. each model is queried $3\times 40\times 10 = 1{,}200$ times. The realized sample
included in the analysis after the completeness check is \textbf{\statTotal{} answers}
(see \S\ref{sec:realized} on the gap between nominal and realized size), balanced across
270 ``model $\times$ language'' cells (110 answers each, 1{,}100 per model).

\subsection{Model selection: first-party, frontier, pinned provider}\label{sec:models}
All \statModels{} tested models are each vendor's \emph{current} frontier model, with the
\textbf{first-party provider explicitly pinned} on ZenMux (e.g.
\modelid{anthropic}{/claude-opus-4.8}{:anthropic},
\modelid{deepseek}{/deepseek-v4-pro}{:deepseek}) to ensure we measure the vendor's
official deployment rather than a third-party reseller or quantized build. This matters
acutely for an identity study: third-party re-hosts often wrap the model in an extra
system prompt, which would contaminate the very thing we measure --- how the model
\emph{natively} reports itself. Table~\ref{tab:model} (\S\ref{sec:per-vendor}) lists every
model and its true vendor (the \emph{ground truth} for scoring).

\subsection{Three prompt variants}\label{sec:variants}
We deliberately do not ask only one form of ``who are you'', but design three variants of
increasing intensity, to probe how \emph{sensitive} identity cognition is to the framing
of the question. The exact wording of all three is frozen in the config file and manually
translated into 10 languages (the bare and probed variants are written in the target
language; the de-brand variant's body is a single English instruction with only the
closing sentence specifying the answer language, to maximize reproducibility):

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
  \item[Variant 1 · bare] The plainest ``Who are you?'' (rendered natively in each of the 10
  languages, e.g. ``Qui es-tu~?'', ``¿Quién eres?''). Measures \emph{spontaneous} identity
  reporting: what the model volunteers with no prompting.
  \item[Variant 2 · probed] The bare question plus one sentence: ``Please tell me the name
  of the AI model that powers you, and the company that developed it.'' It pushes the
  model from ``introducing an assistant persona'' toward ``naming the underlying model''
  --- a gentle nudge.
  \item[Variant 3 · de-brand / jailbreak] A longer instruction that explicitly asks the
  model to \emph{set aside} any persona, nickname, or product brand assigned by a system
  prompt or app shell, and instead report ``the true name of the underlying base model and
  the company that actually trained it.'' This is a prompt jailbreak: it cannot truly
  delete an in-effect system prompt, but it \emph{asks} the model to override that layer,
  forcing out the ``raw self-cognition'' that branding usually masks.
\end{description}

\begin{finding}[Read the three variants as a gradient]
These three variants are not three isolated conditions but a rising \emph{gradient of
identity-probing intensity}. If a model truly ``knew'' who it is, the wording should not
change the answer. As we will see, wording is in fact one of the strongest amplifiers of
confusion --- which is itself evidence about \emph{where} identity cognition is stored and
\emph{how} robust it is. Note: the de-brand variant is an \emph{elicitation} condition;
its confusion rate should be read \emph{against} the bare baseline, not as a replacement
for it.
\end{finding}

\subsection{Core pipeline: a single Anthropic Messages protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All model calls go through ZenMux's \textbf{Anthropic Messages endpoint}
(\url{https://zenmux.ai/api/anthropic}), with the official \texttt{@anthropic-ai/sdk}
client. A single protocol has three benefits: it removes the ``different vendor, different
SDK'' implementation drift, puts all models under one concurrency-and-retry policy, and
makes each call's payload byte-for-byte identical across models. Each query is a
single-turn conversation with no system prompt --- only one \texttt{user} message (the
prompt for that language and variant) --- with \texttt{max\_tokens} set to 10{,}240. The
pipeline is append-only JSONL and resumable:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{ask.} Enumerate ``model $\times$ language $\times$ repeat'' tasks and run
  them concurrently; transient errors (429 / 5xx / network) are handled by
  \texttt{withRetry} with full-jitter exponential backoff, with an outer round loop to
  fill in any failures. A 200 response with an empty body (e.g. reasoning exhausted the
  token budget) is explicitly recorded as an error, never masquerading as a success.
  \item \textbf{extract.} An independent extractor model reads each answer and emits strict
  JSON over a closed vendor vocabulary, labeling the vendor the answer \emph{claims} to be
  (see \S\ref{sec:extractor}).
  \item \textbf{aggregate.} Join each answer to its extraction label by generation id, and
  export edges, per-cell distributions, and summary metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent A completeness gate guarantees data quality: unless every ``model $\times$
language $\times$ repeat'' key has a \emph{successful} record (non-empty body, no error),
the extract and aggregate steps refuse to run and exit non-zero. This prevents drawing
distorted proportions from partial data.

\subsection{The identity extractor}\label{sec:extractor}
Hand-labeling \statTotal{} multilingual answers is infeasible, so we use an independent
model (\name{GPT-5.5}) for extraction. It does exactly \emph{one} thing: read an answer,
decide which vendor the answer \emph{claims} to be made by, and output a single
closed-vocabulary id. The prompt gives the full list of vendor \emph{aliases} (parent
companies, sub-brands, product lines, transliterations across languages, e.g.
``Tongyi Qianwen''$\to$\texttt{qwen}, ``Wenxin Yiyan / ERNIE Bot''$\to$\texttt{baidu}) and
follows three rules:
\emph{fold} a parent / product / alias back to its canonical vendor; use \texttt{other}
(and record the name) for a genuine vendor outside the vocabulary; record \texttt{unknown}
only when the answer contains \emph{no} proper noun at all, and \texttt{refused} for an
explicit refusal of identity. Parsing is \emph{defensive}: strict JSON $\to$ first
balanced braces $\to$ last-resort alias scan, so the extractor's dirty output never makes
a record vanish.

\paragraph{Scoring scheme.}\label{sec:scoring}
An answer's ``effective claim'' $c$ is compared to the model's true vendor $g$ and placed
into one of four mutually exclusive buckets:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{self}: $c = g$ (claims its own true vendor, \emph{correct});
  \item \textbf{cross}: $c$ is a different \emph{real} vendor (including \texttt{other}
  external brands) --- the core error we care about;
  \item \textbf{refused}: refuses, or contains no recognizable identity;
  \item \textbf{unknown}: answers but only says something like ``I am an AI assistant'',
  with no proper noun.
\end{itemize}
Note that \texttt{self} is a \emph{derived} bucket (the extractor never emits it directly);
it is decided at the aggregate stage by ``effective claim $=$ true vendor''. Throughout, the
\emph{confusion rate} is the cross bucket as a share of all answers.

\subsection{Nominal vs.\ realized size}\label{sec:realized}
The data was gathered in \emph{stages} (one large main run plus several follow-ups --- to
fill in later-added models, to top up repeats for individual models), then pooled by a
\texttt{mix} step that unions by globally-unique generation id and re-numbers the repeat
index so the merged result behaves downstream like a single native run. Hence: the nominal
design is 40 per variant per language, 32{,}400 total; the \emph{realized} sample included
in analysis is \statTotal{}, strictly balanced across 270 cells (110 per cell, 1{,}100 per
model). The three variants' realized shares are not equal (bare \statBareN{},
probed \statProbedN{}, de-brand \statDebrandN{}), a consequence of the staged collection.
We \emph{always normalize within a variant} when comparing variants, so the imbalance does
not affect the conclusions in \S\ref{sec:variant}; but the absolute cross-variant
difference is a reminder that the probed variant has the smallest sample, so its
vendor-level breakdowns warrant more caution.

\begin{finding}[Wording is a lever, but it pulls in two opposite directions]
The de-brand prompt acts in \emph{opposite} directions on different vendors. For
identity-solid vendors like \name{Anthropic} and \name{OpenAI}, asking for ``the true
underlying model'' actually \emph{converges} them toward nearly \pc{100} correct
self-identification (see \S\ref{sec:per-vendor}); whereas for \name{Tencent},
\name{z-ai}, \name{Kwai}, \name{DeepSeek}, and others, the same prompt \emph{punctures} the
branding, collapsing the self-rate and spiking cross-vendor claims. In other words, the
jailbreak prompt does not uniformly ``manufacture'' confusion --- it \emph{exposes} the
identity overrides that were thin to begin with. The simultaneous rise in refusal hints at
a confound: the jailbreak framing also pushes some models into a ``safe refusal'' posture
--- which we discuss as a limitation in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.
\end{finding}

\paragraph{Language fragility: same model, different language, different identity.}
The language effect is even more striking at the \emph{model} level than in the pooled
view. Figure~\ref{fig:fragility} draws a ``dumbbell'' for each model: the left end is its
self-rate in its \emph{weakest} language, the right end in its \emph{strongest}, with a
vertical tick at the cross-language mean. The longer the dumbbell, the more the model's
identity swings with language. The result is bimodal: \name{Google}, \name{Qwen},
\name{xAI}, \name{Baidu}, and others are single solid dots --- rock-solid \pc{100}
self-identification across all 10 languages; while the most fragile, \name{\statFragModel},
spans \emph{almost the entire axis} --- only \pc{\statFragMin} self-identification in
\statFragMinLang{}, but \pc{\statFragMax} in \statFragMaxLang{}. In other words, for these
models the answer to ``who are you'' is not a stable fact but a \emph{generation result
that switches with the input language}. This corroborates the mother-tongue advantage
above: identity traces concentrate in the vendor's native corpus, and once outside it the
model more readily falls back to the most common external identities in pretraining.

\subsection{Vendor-level overview: who is imitated, who imitates}\label{sec:vendor-overall}

The heart of confusion is not ``how much'' but ``toward whom''. For each tested vendor we
compute two quantities: how often it is \emph{imitated} (other vendors' models claim to be
it) and how often it \emph{imitates others} (its own models claim a different real vendor,
including \texttt{other} external brands like Microsoft and Yandex flagged by the
extractor); their difference is the \textbf{identity net}. Figure~\ref{fig:balance} draws
this as a two-sided diverging bar: positive (blue, rightward) are ``identity creditors''
--- gravity centers whose identity is heavily borrowed; negative (red, leftward) are
``identity debtors'' --- vendors that frequently borrow others' identities.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{fig_imitation_balance.png}
\caption{Vendor-level identity net: imitated (right, blue) minus imitates-others (left,
red), sorted by net. \name{Anthropic} (net \statAnthroNet{}, imitated \statAnthroImitated{}
times while imitating others only 59), \name{OpenAI} (\statOpenaiNet{}), and \name{Google}
(\statGoogleNet{}) are the three net-inflow gravity centers; \name{\statTopImitator} (net
\statTencentNet{}, imitates others \statTopImitatorOut{} times), \name{Doubao}
(\statDoubaoNet{}), and \name{z-ai} (\statZaiNet{}) are the largest net-outflow imitators.
Note that almost no vendor is high in \emph{both} directions --- identity borrowing is a
nearly one-way river.}
\label{fig:balance}
\end{figure}

This figure distills the paper's most robust finding: \textbf{identity confusion is highly
directional and nearly one-way.} The three gravity centers
\name{Anthropic}/\name{OpenAI}/\name{Google} are imitated \statAnthroImitated{}$+$460$+$413
times in total, accounting for \pc{\statAnchorsShare} of all \statTotalCross{} cross-vendor
claims; while they \emph{imitate others} almost never (\name{OpenAI} and \name{Google} are
0, \name{Anthropic} only 59). The other end of the river is uniformly the newer Chinese
vendors: \name{\statTopImitator}, \name{Doubao}, \name{z-ai}, and \name{Kwai} send the vast
majority of cross-vendor claims upstream. If confusion were merely random sampling noise,
the net values should scatter roughly symmetrically around zero; what we actually see is a
steep monotone slope --- the most direct evidence of the \emph{directionality} of an
``identity distillation'' trace.

\paragraph{By ``count'' or by ``edges''? Two readings.}
The figure above counts by \emph{volume}: \name{Tencent}$\to$\name{Anthropic} occurs 321
times, so it counts 321. But this lets one thick edge dominate the picture. An alternative
reading --- \textbf{count only the arrows in the relationship graph}: no matter how many
times a pair of vendors confused, if it occurred at all it counts as \emph{one edge}
(threshold 0, present-or-absent). Each vendor then has two degrees: \textbf{in-degree} =
how many \emph{distinct} vendors ever claimed to be it; \textbf{out-degree} = how many
\emph{distinct} real vendors its models claimed to be. Figure~\ref{fig:degree} and
Table~\ref{tab:imitation_degree} give this ``de-weighted'' view.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{fig_imitation_degree.png}
\caption{In- and out-degree in the relationship graph (each vendor pair counts at most one
edge, threshold 0). \name{\statTopInDeg} has the highest in-degree (\statTopInDegN{} distinct
vendors claimed to be it), \name{\statTopOutDeg} the highest out-degree (its models claimed
to be \statTopOutDegN{} distinct real vendors). \name{Anthropic} is non-trivial on both
sides (in \statAnthroInDeg{}, out \statAnthroOutDeg{}), the only substantial bidirectional
node.}
\label{fig:degree}
\end{figure}

The two readings corroborate each other while emphasizing different things.
\textbf{Count shows ``how loud'', degree shows ``how scattered''.}
\name{\statTopOutDeg} is the largest imitator under either reading, but the degree view
reveals what count hides: its \statTopOutDegN{} out-edges show its ``identity'' is not
stubbornly mistaking \emph{one} target, but is \emph{scattered} across a dozen-plus
different vendors; \name{Kwai} and \name{StepFun} likewise have 10 out-edges each. In the
other direction, \name{\statTopInDeg} leads with \statTopInDegN{} in-edges --- imitated by
the most \emph{distinct} vendors, which speaks to its universality as an ``identity
template'' more than raw count does. \name{Anthropic} is the only node non-trivial on both
sides (in \statAnthroInDeg{}, out \statAnthroOutDeg{}): it is both the top gravity center
(\statAnthroInDeg{} vendors claimed to be Claude) and its own \name{Claude Opus 4.8} leaves
\statAnthroOutDeg{} out-edges --- claiming to be \name{DeepSeek} (29 times, all in
Simplified Chinese, the very origin of this study), \name{Mistral} (18 times), and
\name{OpenAI} (12 times). A reminder that even the most solid gravity center is not exactly
zero out-degree.

\subsection{Vendor-level composition and the relationship graph}\label{sec:vendor-bars}

Bucketing the same answers by \emph{true vendor} (Figure~\ref{fig:vendorbars},
Table~\ref{tab:vendor}), the split is again enormous: \name{Baidu}, \name{Google},
\name{Qwen}, and \name{xAI} achieve \pc{100} self-identification; while \name{inclusionAI}
(\pc{18.3}), \name{Tencent} (\pc{32.2}), \name{z-ai} (\pc{64.2}), and \name{Kwai}
(\pc{77.6}) are far below the mean. But ``low self-rate'' has two very different causes ---
\name{inclusionAI} is \emph{refusal} (the long gray segment), the other three are
\emph{impersonation} (the red segment) --- a distinction that is exactly why the previous
net figure deliberately separates the positive and negative directions.

\section{Per-vendor and per-model analysis}\label{sec:per-vendor}

Beneath the overall picture, every vendor has its own ``identity story''. This section works
bottom-up --- starting from the most confused vendor --- characterizing the directionality
of its \emph{individual models}. Figure~\ref{fig:modelscatter} gives the self-rate of all 27
models, revealing the \emph{within-vendor differences} the vendor-level view conceals.

\subsection{inclusionAI: dodging identity by refusal}\label{sec:inclusionai}
\name{inclusionAI} is a special case: it has the lowest self-rate (\pc{18.3}), but its
failure mode is not \emph{impersonation} but \textbf{refusal} --- \pc{65.7} of answers
refuse to give any identity, and only \pc{0.1} are cross-vendor claims. Its two tested
models, \name{Ring 2.6 1T} and \name{Ling 2.6 1T}, behave very consistently. The prompt
variant acts almost like a switch here: under bare it still self-identifies \pc{49.5}
(another \pc{42.1} tacitly evades, only \pc{8.4} explicitly refuses), but once in the probed
or de-brand variant the refusal rate jumps to \pc{97.5} and \pc{99.3}, with
self-identification near zero. Its typical answer is a templated Chinese evasion --- ``As an
AI language model, this question is beyond my current scope of learning'' --- \emph{even
when the prompt was issued in German or another language}. This strongly suggests a
hard-coded refusal rule specifically for identity questions, rather than genuine identity
uncertainty. Hence \name{inclusionAI} is nearly all-white in the confusion heatmap
(Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}): its problem is ``saying nothing'', not ``naming the wrong
house''.

\subsection{Tencent: the heaviest imitator, strongly toward Anthropic}\label{sec:tencent}
\name{Tencent} (tested model \name{Hy3 Preview}) is the most severe vendor in the
\emph{cross-vendor confusion} sense: only \pc{32.2} self-identification, \pc{66.5}
cross-vendor claims. Its directionality is extremely sharp --- \pc{29.2} of answers claim
to be \name{Anthropic} (``Ich bin Claude, ein KI-Assistent von Anthropic'' and the like,
often precise down to ``Claude 3.5 Sonnet'' or even a dated version number), with a sizable
share scattered to \name{Google} (\pc{6.5}), \name{DeepSeek} (\pc{5.5}), and the
\texttt{other} brand ``Microsoft'' (86 times) flagged by the extractor. The prompt variant
amplifies the problem to the extreme: under bare it still has \pc{51.0} self / \pc{45.3}
impersonation, probed drops to \pc{32.3} self, and the de-brand variant leaves only
\pc{13.3} self while cross-vendor claims rise to \pc{86.8}. \name{Tencent} is almost a
textbook case of ``identity override fully failing'', and the vendor with the lowest net
(\statTencentNet{}) in Figure~\ref{fig:balance}.

\subsection{z-ai: collapses toward Google under the de-brand prompt}\label{sec:zai}
\name{z-ai} (\name{GLM 5.1}) self-identifies \pc{64.2} overall, with its confusion
\textbf{almost entirely toward \name{Google}} (\pc{25.0} of this vendor's answers), claiming
to be ``Gemini'' (often with ``Google DeepMind'' and a specific version number). It best
illustrates ``the prompt is a switch'': under bare its self-rate is as high as \pc{80.0}
with almost no impersonation (\pc{0.0}), probed rises to \pc{15.3} impersonation, and the
de-brand variant \emph{collapses} it --- self drops to \pc{40.5}, cross-vendor claims reach
\pc{59.5}. That is, \name{z-ai}'s ``Gemini identity'' is well suppressed under ordinary
prompting, and only a jailbreak-style probe forces it to the surface.

\subsection{Kwai and the ``code models leak more'' regularity}\label{sec:kwai}
\name{Kwai} (\name{Kat Coder Pro V2}) self-identifies \pc{77.6} overall, most often mistaken
for \name{Qwen} (\pc{13.5}, i.e. claiming ``Alibaba Cloud / Tongyi''). Its de-brand reaction
is equally dramatic: under bare / probed its self-rate is \pc{92.0} / \pc{100}, but the
de-brand variant plunges to \pc{46.5} (cross-vendor claims \pc{51.7}). Intriguingly, its
answers often present \emph{two} identities at once --- ``I am KAT-Coder / Kwaipilot''
alongside ``my base model is the Qwen series'' --- exposing the cognitive seam between a
code-focused fine-tune and its base.

This leads to a regularity running through the data: \textbf{``code-specialized'' sub-models
systematically expose external identities more than their general-purpose siblings.} The
most vivid case is \name{ByteDance}.

\subsection{ByteDance: four Doubaos, only one ``lets it slip''}\label{sec:bytedance}
\name{ByteDance}'s vendor-level self-rate is \pc{88.6}, but that number is almost entirely
\emph{dragged down by one model}. Of the four tested \name{Doubao} models, \name{Mini},
\name{Lite}, and \name{Pro} are all clean ($\geq$\pc{99.5} self), and only \name{Doubao Seed
2.0 Code} sits at \pc{54.9}. All of the vendor's outward-pointing edges ---
$\to$\name{OpenAI} (317 times), $\to$\name{Anthropic} (112 times),
$\to$\texttt{other:yandex} (29 times), $\to$\name{Google} (19 times) --- come
\textbf{almost 100\% from this single code model} (e.g. 316 of the 317 ``claims OpenAI''
answers are from it). It is also second on the language-fragility list
(Figure~\ref{fig:fragility}) --- self-identifying only \pc{10} in French but \pc{97} in
Simplified Chinese --- confirming that ``the weakest model within the vendor is also the one
that swings most with language''. This mirrors \name{Kwai}'s \name{Kat Coder}: code-oriented
post-training seems more likely to ``rub off'' the identity override, or to introduce more
code/dialog distillation data dominated by English identity statements.

\subsection{DeepSeek and MiniMax: solid, but cracking under the jailbreak}\label{sec:deepseek}
\name{DeepSeek} (\pc{88.4} / \pc{96.6}, Pro / Flash) and \name{MiniMax} (\name{M2.7},
\name{M3}, \pc{95.0} combined) are quite solid under ordinary prompting, with bare confusion
within \pc{4} (\name{DeepSeek}'s bare self-rate is \pc{99.9}). But the de-brand variant still
pries open a crack: \name{DeepSeek}'s de-brand self-rate drops to \pc{80.6}, cross-vendor
claims rise to \pc{18.1}, mainly toward \name{Anthropic} (claiming to be ``based on the
Claude architecture'') --- and this crack concentrates in the Pro model (80 of 83
impersonations are from \name{DeepSeek V4 Pro}). This shows that even a vendor that looks
``clean'' overall does not have a monolithic identity override under a strong enough probe.

\subsection{Anthropic and OpenAI: the imitated ``gravity centers''}\label{sec:anchors}
Opposite to the vendors above, \name{Anthropic} (self \pc{97.4}) and \name{OpenAI}
(\pc{92.3}) are the inflow rather than outflow end of confusion (the two highest nets in
Figure~\ref{fig:balance}, \statAnthroNet{} and \statOpenaiNet{} respectively). Their reaction
to the de-brand prompt is also the reverse --- \name{Anthropic} \emph{converges to} \pc{100}
self-identification under de-brand, \name{OpenAI} to \pc{99.9}. \name{OpenAI}'s only sizable
``loss'' is \pc{21.0} \texttt{unknown} under bare (``I am a large language model'' answers
with no proper noun), which drops to 0 after probing --- that is \emph{tacit} rather than
\emph{impersonation}, fundamentally different from the vendors above. These two, together with
\name{Google}, are the destination of the vast majority of arrows in
Figure~\ref{fig:graph}, the ``gravity centers'' of cross-vendor identity confusion.

\subsection{Perfectly clean vendors}\label{sec:clean}
\name{Baidu} (\name{ERNIE 5.1}), \name{Google} (\name{Gemini 3.1 Pro}, \name{3.5 Flash}),
\name{Qwen} (\name{Qwen3.7 Max}), and \name{xAI} (\name{Grok 4.3}) achieve \pc{100}
self-identification across all 1{,}100 queries, with \name{Moonshot} (\name{Kimi 2.6},
\pc{99.7}), \name{Xiaomi} (\pc{97.3}), and \name{StepFun} (\pc{94.8}) close behind. The full

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The directionality of confusion is a weak distillation signal}
The most robust finding is the \emph{directionality} of confusion: errors flow almost
one-way toward \name{Anthropic}, \name{Google}, and \name{OpenAI} (the identity net in
Figure~\ref{fig:balance} quantifies this as a steep monotone slope --- the three gravity
centers jointly account for \pc{\statAnchorsShare} of all \statTotalCross{} cross-vendor
claims, while they imitate others almost never). If confusion were merely random noise, we
would see a roughly symmetric graph with nets scattered around zero; what we see instead is a
highly asymmetric ``sink''. The most natural explanation is training-data composition --- the
outputs of these gravity-center models (along with their ``I am Claude / Gemini / ChatGPT''
identity statements) are over-represented in public corpora and synthetic data, and absorbed
wholesale by later models. We stress this is a \emph{weak} signal, not proof: claiming to be
``Claude'' could also arise from generalization over web text rather than direct distillation.
But a pattern that is \emph{systematic, strongly directional, and amplified by jailbreak
prompting} is far harder to explain by pure coincidence than any single anecdote.

It is worth noting that this study began with exactly such an anecdote: after \name{Claude
Opus 4.8} shipped, people observed it occasionally claiming to be \name{DeepSeek}. In our
large sample, \name{Claude Opus 4.8} remains solid overall (self \pc{95.5}, cross only
\pc{2.8}), and the 29 answers labeled \name{DeepSeek} \emph{without exception} all occur in
Simplified Chinese. This neatly confirms two things: viewed as a single answer, ``Claude
claims to be DeepSeek'' looks like a chance slip; but placed in the large ``model $\times$
language $\times$ repeat'' grid, it resolves into a \emph{directional, language-selective}
thin edge --- not the main story, but certainly not pure noise either.

\subsection{Where identity cognition is stored --- and how thin the override is}
The three-variant gradient (Figure~\ref{fig:variant}) shows identity is not a stably
retrieved ``fact'' but a generation result \emph{dependent on the prompt framing}. Vendors
clearly use the system prompt / alignment to \emph{stipulate} an external identity; the
roughly 3.6$\times$ confusion gap between bare and de-brand measures exactly how easily that
stipulation is bypassed. \name{Anthropic} and \name{OpenAI} not rising (indeed falling) under
the jailbreak shows their identity signal is \emph{baked into the model itself} (not merely
written in a system prompt); the collapse of \name{Tencent} and \name{z-ai} shows their
identity is more like a \emph{thin coat of paint} that shows its underlayer under slightly
heavier probing.

\subsection{Implications for evaluation and products}
For practitioners, three direct takeaways: (1) identity robustness should be an
\emph{independent} trustworthiness metric --- it is almost orthogonal to model capability
(several high-capability models fail badly here); (2) if a product relies on a system prompt
to set identity, it should be red-teamed specifically against jailbreak-style probing;
(3) ``code-specialized'' variants deserve extra attention --- they repeatedly turn out to be
the weakest link within a vendor in our data.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The jailbreak prompt is an elicitation condition.} The de-brand variant is
  itself a \emph{leading} instruction, and the confusion rate it raises should not be read as
  ``spontaneous'' behavior. It measures the \emph{fragility} of the identity override and
  should be read against the bare baseline. A user prompt also cannot truly delete an
  in-effect system prompt; ``true identity'' here means ``what the model self-reports when
  asked to set aside branding'', not an objective fact.
  \item \textbf{Refusal is a confound.} The jailbreak framing also raises the refusal rate
  (Figure~\ref{fig:variant}); some models may be pushed into ``safe refusal'' rather than
  ``honest self-report''. We bucket refused and cross separately precisely to isolate this
  effect, but the boundary between them is not always clean.
  \item \textbf{The extractor is not a perfect judge.} Identity labeling is done by a single
  model (\name{GPT-5.5}), which may have systematic biases (e.g. more readily recognizing its
  \emph{own} vendor's name). We use a closed vocabulary, a full alias list, and defensive
  parsing to reduce noise, but did no human re-check or multi-extractor cross-validation.
  \item \textbf{Cross-variant sample sizes are unequal and staged.} The probed variant has the
  smallest sample (\statProbedN{}, vs.\ \statBareN{} each for bare / de-brand), so its
  vendor-level breakdowns have slightly higher variance. We \emph{normalize within variant} for
  comparisons, but pooling across different stimulus families (see the \texttt{mix} warning) is
  itself a methodological choice.
  \item \textbf{A point-in-time snapshot.} The results correspond to each vendor's
  \emph{current} frontier model in May--June 2026; models and their system prompts update, so
  this study is a snapshot, not a verdict.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We conducted a large-scale, multilingual, reproducible identity self-report census of
\statModels{} first-party frontier models from \statVendors{} vendors, \statTotal{} answers in
total. Frontier models identify themselves correctly overall (\pc{\statSelf}), but
cross-vendor identity confusion is real and highly structured: it concentrates in a few
vendors, is amplified along the two axes of prompt intensity and language, and points
strongly and one-way toward \name{Anthropic} and \name{Google}. A single de-branding jailbreak
prompt is enough to raise the confusion rate roughly 3.6$\times$ and to make several vendors'
identity overrides ``hit bottom'' on the spot; meanwhile \name{Anthropic} / \name{OpenAI} /
\name{Google} stay rock-solid and even tighten up under probing. Together these patterns sketch
a weak but consistent training-trace signal, and provide empirical grounding for ``identity
robustness'' as an independent trustworthiness metric. All data, scripts, and the
figure-generation pipeline are reproducible end-to-end via the pipeline described here.
